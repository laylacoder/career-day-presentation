# Career Day — Talking Points
**Sr. Manager, Data Engineering @ Murmuration**
**Audience:** 8th–9th graders | **Time:** 30 minutes

---

## SLIDE 1 — Title: "From Code to Community Impact"
**Time: ~1 min**

Say something like:
> "Hi everyone — I'm [your name], and I'm a Sr. Manager of Data Engineering at a company called Murmuration.
> Today I want to tell you what that actually means, how I got here, and why — honestly — I think it's one of
> the coolest jobs you can have. And I'll try to do it without putting you to sleep."

**Tips:**
- Smile, make eye contact, don't read off the slide.
- If your daughter is in the room, a quick "shoutout to my daughter for getting me here" lands well with the class.

---

## SLIDE 2 — "How many of these did you use today?"
**Time: ~2 min**

Ask the question on screen — get them raising hands. Make it fun:

> "Keep your hand up the whole time. Spotify or Apple Music today? TikTok? YouTube? Anyone order food on DoorDash
> or Uber Eats? Play any game online?"

> "Every single one of those runs on data pipelines. The Spotify recommendation that got stuck in your head?
> A data engineer built the system that made that possible. The TikTok For You Page that kept you up until 2am?
> Billions of data points, processed in real time, by systems data engineers built."

> "Data engineering is one of the fastest-growing, best-paid fields in all of tech — and most people your age
> have never heard of it. That changes today."

**Tips:**
- This is your energy-setter. Be enthusiastic, move around, make eye contact.
- The hand-raise moment gets them physically engaged from the start.

---

## SLIDE 3 — Hook: "What if the most powerful tool for your community was data?"

**Time: ~2–3 min**

Open with the question on the screen. Pause and actually let them think.

Then say:
> "Think about your neighborhood, your school, any issue you care about — housing, schools, voting rights, whatever.
> Now imagine that the groups fighting for those things are going up against organizations that have millions of dollars
> and the best technology available. Most grassroots community groups? They have neither. That's the gap my team helps close."

> "We build the data systems that give community organizations the same powerful tools as the well-funded groups
> they're up against. And that starts with something called data engineering."

**Tips:**
- Make it personal — mention a local or national issue they've likely heard of.
- Pause after the quote on the slide — let it land.

---

## SLIDE 4 — My Journey (Visual Timeline)

**Time: ~4–5 min**

Walk through the timeline left to right — point to each node as you talk:

**Node 1 — Year 1, Software Engineer:**
> "I started as a Software Engineer. That means writing code every day — building features, fixing bugs, shipping products.
> My whole job was: here's a problem, write the code that solves it."

**Node 2 — Year 6–7, Noticing Data:**
> "About six or seven years in, something shifted. I kept noticing that we were collecting all this data and nobody really
> knew what it meant. I started asking questions my colleagues weren't asking — like, 'what does this data actually tell
> us about our users?' That curiosity didn't go away."

**Node 3 — Year 8–10, Data Engineering:**
> "I started specializing while I was still a Software Engineer. I began building data pipelines, designing how data
> flows through systems, making sure it could be trusted. And I loved it — way more than just building features."

**Node 4 — +6 Years, Sr. Data Engineer:**
> "Over the next six years I went deep. I became the person people came to for data architecture. I was mentoring
> other engineers, leading technical decisions, designing systems that had to be reliable at scale."

**Node 5 — Today, Sr. Manager:**
> "And now? I lead the data engineering team at Murmuration. I do it all — strategy, roadmap, architecture, and
> making sure my team has what they need to do their best work."

**Closing the slide:**
> "The thing I want you to notice about this timeline is that it didn't follow a straight line. I didn't decide in
> year one that I'd be a data engineering manager. I followed what I found interesting at each step. That's actually
> the best career advice I can give you."

**Tips:**
- Point physically to each node as you describe it — makes it feel alive.
- Pause on Node 2 (the pivot moment) — that's the most relatable part for teens who don't know what they want yet.
- The "Today" node pulses gold — let it draw attention naturally before you get there.

---

## SLIDE 4 — What is Data Engineering?

**Time: ~4–5 min**

Use the water/pipes analogy before explaining the diagram:

> "Data engineering is hard to explain because it's infrastructure — you don't see it, but everything breaks without it.
> Here's the best analogy I have: think of data like water. It exists everywhere, but it's useless if you can't get it
> to where it needs to go, filtered and clean."

> "Data engineers build the pipes. We take raw, messy data from a dozen different sources — apps, forms, databases,
> spreadsheets — clean it up, organize it, and deliver it reliably so that analysts and decision-makers can actually
> use it."

Point to the diagram and walk through it:
> "Raw data in → pipelines we build → clean, reliable data → insights and action. That last step is where
> someone says 'we should do X' based on what the data shows. We make that possible."

**Potential follow-up if they ask "what's a pipeline?":**
> "A pipeline is just a series of automated steps that move and transform data. Think of it like an assembly line
> for information."

**Tips:**
- Draw on the board if you have one — a simple boxes-and-arrows sketch is very effective.
- Ask: "Has anyone heard of SQL or Python?" — it connects to what they may already know.

---

## SLIDE 5 — Murmuration: Tech for the People

**Time: ~3–4 min**

> "So where do I do this work? At a company called Murmuration. And honestly, it's one of the reasons I love
> what I do."

> "Murmuration is a civic tech organization. We build data tools and platforms specifically for grassroots community
> groups — the kind that are fighting for better schools, higher voter turnout, more equitable policies. The ones that
> usually can't afford the tech that the other side has."

> "My team specifically builds and maintains the data infrastructure that powers all of that. When a community
> organizer logs into our platform and sees which neighborhoods need more outreach, or which voters haven't been
> reached — that's data my team made reliable and accessible."

**Key line:**
> "Tech isn't just about making the next app or getting rich. It can be a direct tool for justice. That matters to me."

**Tips:**
- If any students have been involved in activism, youth groups, or school advocacy — this will resonate.
- You don't need to be political. Keep the focus on: "giving people access to tools."

---

## SLIDE 6 — A Day in My Life @ Murmuration

**Time: ~3–4 min**

Bust the myth that managers just go to meetings:

> "People assume that once you become a manager, you stop doing technical work. At Murmuration, that's not true
> for me — and honestly, I think that's what makes me better at both jobs."

Walk through each card:

> "I still write code and do code reviews. When I review a teammate's pull request, I'm not just checking if it
> works — I'm thinking about whether it's maintainable, scalable, and aligned with where we're going."

> "I lead architectural decisions — when we're building a new data system, I'm the one helping design how it's
> structured. That means thinking years ahead, not just this sprint."

> "Sprint planning — every two weeks, I run a session where the team decides what we're working on, in what order,
> and why. It's part project management, part prioritization, part negotiation."

> "Unblocking. This is huge. If someone on my team is stuck — waiting on another team, unclear on requirements,
> hit a technical wall — my job is to go remove that obstacle so they can keep moving."

> "And strategy and roadmap — I create the long-term plan. What are we building this year? How does our work
> connect to Murmuration's mission? I'm the one who has to answer those questions and communicate them clearly."

**Key message:**
> "The best managers in tech don't fully stop being engineers. You lead better when you understand the work — and
> your team trusts you more when they know you've been in the trenches."

**Tips:**
- Share a real example of unblocking your team or making an architectural call if time allows.
- This is a great place to show that "manager" doesn't mean "no longer technical."

---

## SLIDE 7 — From Code to Leadership

**Time: ~3–4 min**

This is a great slide for being real with them:

> "Here's something nobody told me when I was in school: one of the hardest parts of moving into leadership
> isn't learning new technical skills. It's learning how to communicate, listen, and support other people."

> "When I was an engineer, I measured my success by the quality of my code. Did it work? Did it scale?
> Was it clean? Now I measure my success by whether my team is growing, whether they feel supported, and
> whether the work we ship together actually makes an impact."

> "The surprise? Empathy is a technical skill in leadership. How you listen, how you give feedback, how you
> run a meeting — it all matters."

**Key message for teens:**
> "If you want to lead someday — and you don't have to, it's a choice — start working on your communication
> and your ability to understand other people's perspectives now. That's not soft stuff. It's hard stuff."

**Tips:**
- This often surprises students who think STEM = no soft skills needed.
- Be vulnerable here — share a moment where a people challenge was harder than a technical one.

---

## SLIDE 8 — How You Could Get Here

**Time: ~3–4 min**

Make this practical and encouraging:

> "Let me tell you what actually matters if you want to go into tech or data."

Walk through the left column:
> "Math — not because you'll be doing calculus every day, but because it trains your brain to think logically
> and systematically. Statistics especially. When you see a data point, you want to ask: is this actually
> meaningful, or just noise?"

> "Computer Science — if your school offers it, take it. If not, no problem. Python and SQL are the two most
> important languages in data engineering, and you can start learning both for free today."

> "Writing and communication — I know, sounds weird for a tech job. But I spend probably 40% of my day
> writing — emails, docs, proposals, Slack messages. Being clear in writing is a superpower in any job."

Walk through the right column:
> "Here are four free resources. I'd start with Codecademy for Python — you can do your first lesson in
> under an hour. No experience needed."

**Key line:**
> "You don't need a fancy school, a rich family, or a computer science class to start. You need curiosity
> and internet access. Most of you have both."

**Tips:**
- Write the resource names on the board if possible.
- Ask: "Has anyone already tried any coding?" — get the room engaged.

---

## SLIDE 9 — Data Engineering Changes Real Lives

**Time: ~2–3 min**

Don't dwell here too long — let the visuals do the work. Keep it punchy:

> "I want to leave you with this: data engineering isn't abstract. It affects real people."

> "When a community group uses our platform to identify which voters haven't been contacted and sends
> volunteers to those exact doors — that's our data pipelines working. When a parent coalition has the
> information they need to fight for better school funding — that's us."

> "The code my team writes, the pipelines we build — they're in service of communities that don't always
> have a voice. That's what gets me out of bed."

**Tips:**
- This is your emotional anchor slide. Speak slowly and with conviction.
- It's okay to be personal here — why does *this* work matter to you?

---

## SLIDE 10 — Q&A

**Time: ~5–7 min**

Kick it off rather than waiting awkwardly:

> "Okay, I want to hear from you. I've talked enough. What questions do you have — about tech, about data,
> about my job, or anything else?"

**Great answers to likely questions:**

**"What's the salary?"**
> "Legitimate question. Data engineers are in high demand. Entry-level is typically $80–100K+. Senior engineers
> and managers often make $150K or more depending on the company and location. It's one of the better-paying
> fields in tech."

**"Do you have to be good at math?"**
> "You need to think logically, and some stats helps. But you don't need to be a math genius. Persistence and
> problem-solving matter more than raw math ability."

**"What if I don't want to be a manager? Can I just code?"**
> "Absolutely. Individual contributor roles — senior engineers, staff engineers, principal engineers — are
> amazing careers. Management is one path, not the only path."

**"What do you wish you'd known in 8th or 9th grade?"**
> "That the skills that matter most aren't on a test. Curiosity, resilience, and the ability to keep learning
> when things get hard — those outlast any grade you'll ever get."

**Tips:**
- Have your contact info or LinkedIn ready if students want to follow up.
- Thank them genuinely at the end — 8th graders who stay engaged for 30 minutes deserve credit.

---

## Closing Line (after Q&A)

> "Thank you. You asked great questions. If any of you ever want to talk more about tech careers, data, or
> anything I mentioned today — find me, or ask [daughter's name] to connect you. Good luck this year."

## SLIDE 10 — Code Demo: Stage 1 Data Engineering
**Time: ~4–5 min**

Start by pointing to the pipeline diagram at the top:

> "Before I walk through the code, I want to show you where my team sits in the bigger picture."

> "Think of this as an assembly line for data. Raw data comes in from all kinds of sources — spreadsheets,
> databases, APIs. My team is Stage 1: we ingest that raw data and transform it into something clean,
> reliable, and structured. Then we hand it off to downstream systems, which use it to power APIs and
> ultimately the frontend application that community organizers actually log into."

> "Without Stage 1, nothing downstream works. We're the foundation."

Now walk through the **before/after tables**:

> "Look at the 'Raw Input' table on the right. See the problems? 'YES' in caps, 'ny' in lowercase, a missing
> name, a zip code with only 3 digits. This is real — data from the real world is almost always dirty."

> "After our pipeline runs — look at the 'Transformed Output.' Names are consistent, zip codes are zero-padded
> to 5 digits, state codes are uppercase, bad rows are dropped. That's the transformation my team does."

Now walk through the **code**:

> "The code tells that same story. We pull the raw file, clean and normalize every column, then filter to find
> who hasn't been contacted yet. At the very end — we write the result out as a file called a parquet file,
> which is a format downstream systems can read super efficiently."

> "That last line — 'Transformed data ready for downstream' — that's the handoff. Our job is done.
> Now the API team, the analysts, the frontend — they all get clean, reliable data to build on."

Point to the **bar chart**:

> "And this chart is what the output looks like visually — the top zip codes where the most people still
> haven't been reached. A community organizer opens their dashboard and sees exactly where to send volunteers.
> That dashboard is powered by the file our pipeline just wrote."

**Key message:**
> "My team doesn't build the apps or the models or the APIs. We build the clean, trusted data that makes
> all of those things possible. Data scientists need it to train models. Product teams need it to understand
> users. The API team needs it to serve reliable data to the application. If our data is wrong or late,
> everything downstream breaks. That's the responsibility — and the importance — of Stage 1 data engineering."

**Tips:**
- Point to the pipeline flow diagram when describing the handoff.
- Highlight the red cells in the raw table — visual contrast makes the "before" very obvious.
- Ask: "Does anyone see what's wrong with that raw data?" — let them find the issues themselves.
